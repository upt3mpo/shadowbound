Chapter V

The Social Cost

The official record said Sir Albrecht Vane, Mira of the Dawn Choir, and Tovan Vale died sealing the Spires Below the Basilica.

It did not mention Ilyas.

The Church understood the value of omission. It let them keep their miracle while burying the evidence of its cost.

High Inquisitor Vareen told Ilyas the dead would be honored. Their names would be sung at morning rites. Their families would be paid. The Church was practiced at turning grief into ceremony.

“And me?” Ilyas asked.

Vareen looked at him for a long moment. She was old enough to have mistaken many things for necessity and wise enough to know when she was doing it again. That did not stop her.

“You will continue your work,” she said.

So he did.

At first, he tried to manage the danger.

He kept distance during briefings. He stood near open air when possible. He refused attendants and escorts. When people reached for him, he stepped away before they could touch cloth.

It worked. For a time.

Then the city filled.

Karthain had become a bottleneck. Refugees pressed against its walls. Supplies ran thin. Patrols failed to return. Every day, more people arrived than the city could feed.

Distance became a luxury no one could afford.

Distance is mercy. Mercy requires space.

The first time he used the Veil inside the crowd, it felt different.

Not stronger. Cleaner.

There was so much life packed into so little space that the Veil did not need to reach. It simply drew.

The Husks at the perimeter slowed before they reached the barricades. Guards held their lines longer. Fewer people died.

Inside the walls, healers reported fatigue they could not explain.

Ilyas heard the reports. He adjusted his position. He stepped farther from the infirmary. He told himself it was enough.

It was not.

In the inn near the western gate, a girl brought him bread. She was young, flour still dusted along her cheek, trying to be useful in a city that had no place for children anymore.

She returned the next morning unable to stand.

The healer blamed exhaustion. Ilyas left before the explanation could reach him.

On the northern road, a pilgrim named Odran shared his fire without asking questions. He recognized Ilyas and pretended not to. That kindness made the silence between them bearable.

By dawn, Odran’s beard was rimed with frost. He was alive, but barely. Ilyas placed three silver coins beside him and walked before the man woke.

After that, he avoided shelter. Ruins. Empty chapels. Broken towers. Places where nothing living would remain long enough to be harmed, and nothing kind would have time to make him stay.

His loneliness changed shape over time. At first it was punishment. Then discipline. Then habit.

Eventually, it became preference.

Alone, he did not have to soften his casting. Alone, he did not have to count breaths that were not his. Alone, he could end things quickly.

He told himself this was mercy.

And perhaps it was. But there was relief in it too, and relief was harder to forgive than guilt. Guilt still loved the people it had failed. Relief was already learning how to live without them.

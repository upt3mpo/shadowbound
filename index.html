Chapter I

The Bell That Would Not Ring

Before the Veil, Father Ilyas believed holiness was measured by distance. The righteous did not stand above the suffering. They stood beside it. They entered fever rooms, plague alleys, battlefields, and funeral houses. They put their hands where others would not look, and they trusted the Light to pass through them without judgment.

The Cathedral of Dawn had been built around that belief. Its nave was wide enough for the sick to sleep between the pews. Its doors were never barred, even in winter. Its bells rang every morning before sunrise, not to summon the faithful, but to remind the starving that someone still knew they were alive.

On the morning the bells failed, Ilyas was hearing confession from a woman named Selka, whose son had been bitten by something that came out of the river fog. She had not come to ask forgiveness. She had come to ask whether mercy could still be given to a child who no longer recognized his own mother.

Ilyas had begun to answer when the first scream tore through the cathedral.

It did not sound like fear. Fear was human. This was the sound of a throat being used by something that had never needed one before.

He ran into the nave and found the congregation folding in on itself. Parishioners clawed at their own skin as black veins surfaced beneath their faces. A mason named Jorren slammed his head against a pillar until the stone cracked. Two choirboys tried to hold down Sister Ameline while her spine arched so violently that her heels touched the back of her skull.

At the center of it all stood Selka’s son.

The boy was seven. Ilyas remembered the baptism. He remembered the child laughing because the water had been cold. Now the boy’s jaw hung open too far, and something black and wet moved behind his teeth.

“Father,” the boy said, though three voices spoke with him.

Ilyas lifted his hand and called the Light.

It came, as it always had. Gold fire burst from his palm and struck the child in the chest. For one terrible instant, the boy looked human again. His eyes cleared. His mouth shaped a word that may have been mother.

Then he became ash.

The nave erupted. Some parishioners fled toward the doors. Others fell to their knees and prayed. The corrupted among them moved faster than dying bodies should have moved, hurling themselves at the living with the boneless desperation of drowning men.

Ilyas fought from the aisle to the altar, one burst of Light at a time. He severed corruption from flesh when he could. When he could not, he burned both together. The cathedral filled with heat and screaming. Wax ran down the candles in thick streams. Blood steamed on the marble.

Selka crawled through the chaos, clutching the ash that had been her son. A Husked Devout saw her move and turned.

Ilyas had enough strength to save her or to hold the eastern doors where twenty civilians were trapped behind fallen pews.

He chose the doors.

By the time the bells finally rang, they were not being pulled by hands. They swung in the wind from the broken tower, tolling over a courtyard filled with the dead.

That evening, the survivors called him blessed. They kissed his sleeves. They thanked him for saving the cathedral.

Ilyas looked at the blackened shape of Selka near the altar steps and understood something that would never leave him.

The Light was merciful.

It was not enough.

Chapter II

The Veil Beneath the Altar

The dead were still being counted when Ilyas descended beneath the cathedral.

No one ordered him to go. No one knew the lower crypt existed except the senior clergy, and most of them had died in the first hour of the attack. The entrance had been hidden beneath the reliquary of Saint Orven, a martyr famous for sealing a wound in the world by singing until his lungs filled with blood.

Ilyas moved the reliquary himself. The saint’s bones rattled inside their silver case as the stone slab beneath scraped open.

The stairs below did not smell of dust. They smelled of rain on old iron.

He carried no torch. The Light gathered around his fingers, but it seemed thinner there, less certain. It touched the walls and drew back. Old warnings had been carved into the stone in several dead languages. Ilyas knew only one of them.

Do not mistake answer for grace.

At the bottom of the stairwell, the crypt opened into a circular chamber. The floor had split down the center, not cracked by age or pressure, but parted like lips. Black vapor rose from the fracture in slow ribbons. It curled toward Ilyas as he approached.

The scriptures called the Veil an absence. Darkness without purpose. Hunger without shape.

That was the first lie he recognized.

The Veil was not empty. It was listening.

Ilyas stood over the fracture and felt a presence consider him. Not a demon. Not a god. Something quieter and older than worship. It did not promise power. It did not seduce him with visions. It simply showed him the cathedral as it had been that morning, then showed it again with every Husk dead before it touched a civilian.

He saw Selka alive.

He saw the child die faster.

He saw himself standing in the center of it all, untouched and terrible.

“No,” he whispered.

The Veil did not answer.

It did not need to. Ilyas had already begun bargaining with the vision. Not because he wanted darkness. Not because he hated the Light. Because he had seen the shape of a better outcome, and it had cost him nothing yet.

He knelt and placed his hand inside the fracture.

Cold entered him without violence. It moved through his skin, his blood, his breath. It filled every hollow place grief had opened. For a moment, he thought he heard every candle in the cathedral above extinguish at once.

Then the cold settled around his heart like a second set of ribs.

Chapter III

The First Borrowing

The second incursion came three nights later, when the city had not yet finished digging graves for the first.

This time the attack began in the plague district, where families slept six to a room and the alleys were too narrow for carts. Ilyas arrived with six wardens, two healers, and a crowd of desperate volunteers carrying farm tools and kitchen knives. They looked to him because he wore white vestments and because survivors had already begun to exaggerate what he had done at the cathedral.

He told them to stay behind him.

The first Husk came through a doorway on all fours. It had once been a butcher. Ilyas recognized the apron before he recognized the face. Its arms had lengthened, fingers dragging against the stones, nails split and sharpened into hooks.

Ilyas raised his right hand to call the Light.

His left moved first.

Shadow slid out of him in a thin, soundless thread. It reached the butcher before the creature could spring. The Husk froze. Its mouth opened. Black vapor poured from its eyes, nose, and throat, pulled into Ilyas like smoke into a chimney.

The body collapsed without flame.

No ash. No explosion. No wasted motion.

The volunteers stared.

Ilyas stared with them.

He felt no exhaustion. Quite the opposite. The corruption entered him and became strength. The ache in his shoulder from moving Saint Orven’s reliquary vanished. The cut across his palm sealed. His breathing steadied.

Another Husk came. Then three. Then a dozen from the alley mouth.

Ilyas pulled again.

This time the shadow spread wider. It caught the front line of creatures and unraveled them where they stood. Their bodies sank inward, ribs collapsing, faces drying into leather. The wardens cheered. One of the healers began to laugh in disbelief.

Then a volunteer behind Ilyas dropped to one knee.

His name was Bren. He was seventeen, maybe eighteen, with a rusted spear and a strip of red cloth tied around his wrist. Ilyas had seen him earlier helping an old man across the square.

Bren coughed, wiping blood from his nose.

“I’m fine,” the boy said before anyone asked.

Ilyas felt the truth before he understood it. The shadow had not only drawn from the Husks. It had brushed everything living nearby. A sliver from each. A breath. A pulse. A little warmth taken without permission.

More Husks appeared at the alley mouth.

Bren was still coughing.

Ilyas could stop. He could fall back and force the wardens to hold the line while he returned to the slower mercy of Light.

Instead, he reached deeper.

The alley died.

When the last Husk fell, Bren was lying on his side, eyes open, red cloth dark around his wrist. The healers said his heart had failed from fear. The wardens repeated it because it was easier.

Ilyas said nothing.

That night, the district survived. Mothers pressed their children to their chests and wept his name like a prayer. Men who had doubted him bowed when he passed.

Ilyas returned to the cathedral and washed Bren’s blood from the hem of his vestments.

It took longer than expected.

Chapter IV

The Party That Faded

The Church refused to abandon Ilyas because abandoning him would have required admitting they had lost control of their own miracle.

Instead, they assigned him companions.

Sir Albrecht was first among them, a broad man with a sunburst shield and a habit of speaking only when silence became impractical. Mira was a novice hymnkeeper whose voice could steady frightened soldiers and soothe fevered children. Tovan was a mercenary who claimed he had fought in nine wars, though he was young enough that at least three of them had to be lies.

They met Ilyas at the sealed gate beneath the ruined east transept.

“We are not here to watch you,” Albrecht said.

“Of course not,” Tovan added, tightening the strap on his sword belt. “We are here to make sure the priest does not heroically die before anyone important can take credit.”

Mira gave him a sharp look. “That is not why we are here.”

“It is partly why we are here.”

Ilyas almost smiled. Almost.

Their objective was simple in the way doomed things were often simple. Something beneath the basilica was gathering the bodies of the dead and making a choir out of them. Survivors reported hearing hymns from below, sung backward through broken throats. Every hour the sound grew louder. Every hour more corpses disappeared from the grave trenches.

They entered the catacombs at dusk.

The first passage was ordinary enough: old stone, low ceilings, alcoves filled with bones. Then the walls began to change. Mortar softened into dark tissue. Roots as thin as veins threaded through the cracks. When Mira brushed one by accident, it recoiled.

“Something is breathing,” Tovan said.

“Everything here is dead,” Albrecht replied.

Tovan drew his sword. “That has not been as reassuring as you think.”

The first corpse did not rise dramatically. It shifted. A shoulder rolled. A jaw clicked. Its spine bent too far backward, then snapped upright with a wet correction.

Tovan moved before anyone could stop him. He drove his blade through the corpse’s chest and pinned it to the wall.

The corpse looked down at the sword, then at him.

Its ribs opened.

The bones spread like fingers and closed around Tovan’s forearm. He screamed, not from pain at first, but from the insult of being held by something that should not have been strong enough to hold him.

Albrecht slammed his shield into the corpse, crushing it against the wall. Ilyas struck with Light at the same moment, and the thing burst apart in a rain of ash and black fluid.

Tovan staggered back, clutching his arm. Bone-white scratches marked his skin in a perfect ring.

“Still attached,” he said, trying to grin. “Good sign.”

No one answered.

They pushed deeper. At every junction, the backward hymn grew clearer. It had melody but no breath, rhythm but no life. Mira began singing under her breath to counter it. Her hymn put warmth into the air, and for a while the walls stopped pulsing when she passed.

Then the catacombs opened.

They found a chamber the size of the cathedral nave, though no such space should have existed beneath the city. Coffins hung from the ceiling by cords of black sinew. Bodies had been arranged along the walls in ascending rows, their mouths sewn open with wire. At the center of the chamber stood Malgeroth, Choir of Rot.

It had no single body. It wore many. Torso upon torso fused around a central column of bone, faces turning in different directions, all singing the same reversed prayer. Its arms were made of arms. Its crown was a ring of infant skulls polished clean.

Mira stopped singing.

Malgeroth sang louder.

The corpses on the walls opened their eyes.

Albrecht moved first. He planted his shield and shouted for the others to form behind him. Tovan took the left flank, bleeding arm held close but sword ready. Mira recovered enough to begin a hymn of binding, her voice fragile at first, then stronger as Light gathered around her like dawn through fog.

Ilyas stood at the rear and cast Radiant Severance into the first wave.

It worked. For a time, it worked beautifully.

Light carved through the undead as Albrecht broke their charge. Tovan hacked the legs from anything that slipped around the shield. Mira’s hymn slowed the Rot spreading across the floor, forcing it back in golden ripples. They were four bodies moving toward one purpose. For the first time since the cathedral fell, Ilyas remembered what faith had felt like before it became arithmetic.

Then Malgeroth changed the song.

The hymn became a wet grinding sound. The Rot surged across the chamber and climbed Albrecht’s shield. It crawled over Tovan’s boots. It entered Mira’s voice and bent one note sour.

Albrecht grunted as black lines spread up his wrist.

“Cleanse!” he shouted.

Ilyas turned the Light on him. Gold flame burned the corruption away, but the effort cost time. In that time, three corpses reached Mira. Tovan intercepted two. The third struck her across the face and sent her to the ground.

Ilyas burned the third. Albrecht nearly fell while he did.

The chamber forced a rhythm on them. Cleanse Albrecht, save Mira. Save Mira, lose ground. Burn the dead, let the Rot spread. Stop the Rot, let Malgeroth sing.

They were not losing because they lacked courage.

They were losing because courage had become inefficient.

Ilyas felt the Veil behind his ribs. Not urging. Not whispering. Waiting.

Mira looked back at him from the floor, blood running from her nose. “Father, please.”

He thought she was asking for help.

Later, he would wonder if she had already understood and was asking him not to.

Ilyas lowered his right hand.

Albrecht saw it. “No.”

Shadow spread from Ilyas’s feet in a perfect circle.

It did not explode. It claimed. The gold light in the room dimmed and inverted. Mira’s hymn twisted into a note too low for human lungs. The undead froze as if listening to a command from beneath the world.

Then they collapsed.

Malgeroth’s many mouths screamed as the Cathedral of Dusk took shape. The bodies fused into its mass shriveled one by one, souls or shadows or whatever remained of them pulled loose in black strands. The Rot recoiled from the floor. Coffins fell from the ceiling and burst open. The chamber became a storm of dust, bone, and devoured song.

For three heartbeats, Ilyas believed he had saved them.

Then Tovan coughed blood across his own hands.

Mira’s hymn stopped.

Albrecht turned from the dying boss and forced his way toward Ilyas. Every step looked impossible. His armor blackened. The sunburst on his shield cracked down the center.

“End it,” Albrecht said.

Ilyas tried.

He reached for the edge of the spell and found none. Cathedral of Dusk was not a cast. It was a condition. Once invited, the Veil did not distinguish between enemy, ally, martyr, or friend. It recognized only nearness.

Tovan fell first. He dropped to one knee, tried to make a joke, and died before finding one.

Mira crawled toward Ilyas. Her eyes were wide, not accusing, only terrified by the betrayal of her own body. Frost whitened her lips.

“Father,” she whispered, “why am I cold?”

Albrecht reached her and tried to lift her away from the circle. His gauntlets passed under her arms. For one moment, it seemed he might succeed.

Then his armor hit the ground empty.

The spell ended when there was nothing left close enough to feed it.

Malgeroth was gone. The Rot was gone. The catacombs were silent.

Ilyas stood untouched at the center of the chamber, surrounded by victory.

Chapter V

The Social Cost

The official record said Sir Albrecht, Mira of the Dawn Choir, and Tovan Vale died sealing the Spires Below the Basilica.

It did not mention Ilyas.

The Church understood the value of omission. It let them keep their miracle while burying the evidence of its cost. High Inquisitor Vareen told Ilyas the dead would be honored. Their names would be sung at morning rites. Their families would be paid.

“And me?” Ilyas asked.

Vareen looked at him for a long moment. She was old enough to have mistaken many things for necessity and wise enough to know when she was doing it again.

“You will continue your work,” she said.

So he did.

At first, he tried to manage the danger. He kept distance during briefings. He stood near open windows. He refused squires, attendants, and escorts. When people insisted on touching his sleeve, he stepped away before their fingers could find cloth.

The precautions helped until they did not.

In the town of Merrowick, an innkeeper offered him a private room and hot stew. Ilyas accepted because it had been raining for six days and he had not slept under a roof since leaving Karthain. The innkeeper’s daughter brought him bread. She was perhaps twelve, with flour on her cheek and the careful seriousness of a child trusted with adult work.

She returned the next morning unable to stand.

The healer blamed winter fever. Ilyas left before the fever could spread to the explanation.

On the north road, a pilgrim named Odran shared his fire without asking questions. He recognized Ilyas and pretended not to. That kindness made the silence between them bearable. They ate salted fish while wolves howled somewhere beyond the trees.

By dawn, Odran’s beard was rimed with frost. He was alive, but barely. Ilyas placed three silver coins beside him and walked until the sun rose high enough to make shadows short.

After that, he slept in ruins, caves, burned chapels, and once inside the ribcage of something large enough that he chose not to identify it.

His loneliness changed shape over time. At first it was punishment. Then discipline. Then habit.

Eventually, it became preference.

Alone, he did not have to soften his casting. Alone, he did not have to count breaths that were not his. Alone, he could enter a demon nest and erase it without worrying who might be standing too close when the Veil opened its mouth.

He told himself this was mercy.

And perhaps it was.

But there was relief in it too, and relief was harder to forgive than guilt.

Chapter VI

The Rift Over Karthain

The sky opened above Karthain at noon.

There was no thunder, no omen, no priestly dream the night before. The sun simply tore down the middle, and behind it waited a darkness with depth. People standing in the market square saw stars where daylight had been. Then they saw something moving between them.

The first wave of Voidborne fell like burning insects. They struck rooftops, towers, streets, and gardens, unfolding from impact craters into tall, jointed things with blades where faces should have been. The city guard formed ranks. Bells rang. Civilians fled toward the cathedral because fear remembers old habits even after faith has been wounded.

Ilyas watched from a hill beyond the western road.

He had not come to Karthain. He had been avoiding it. The city was too full, too loud, too alive. Yet there it was below him, screaming beneath a wound in heaven.

High Inquisitor Vareen found him before sunset. Her horse collapsed ten paces away, foam at its mouth, flanks trembling from a ride that should have taken half a day.

Vareen did not waste breath on accusation.

“Lead us,” she said.

Behind her, on the road, stood soldiers, wardens, surviving clergy, and volunteers too frightened to be brave but too decent to run. They carried spears, lanterns, relics, and hope. Ilyas could feel their lives like candle flames in a draft.

Too many. Too close.

“No,” he said.

Vareen’s expression did not change. “If the Rift widens, the capital falls.”

“If I stand among them, they die before reaching the gate.”

“Then control it.”

Ilyas looked at the soldiers. Some recognized him. Some made warding signs. One young woman smiled at him with such desperate gratitude that he had to look away.

“That is what everyone asks before they learn what control means,” he said.

Vareen left him there.

The army marched without him.

From the hill, Ilyas watched them enter the burning outskirts. They fought well. Better than he expected. Vareen led from the front, her silver mace breaking Voidborne limbs while priests behind her raised barriers of Light. For one hour, perhaps two, it seemed possible that courage and formation and old magic might be enough.

Then the thing behind the Rift noticed them.

The second wave did not fall. It emerged.

Long arms reached through the tear and placed pieces of itself into the city. Streets folded. Buildings twisted inward. Soldiers who had been standing beside one another were suddenly separated by impossible distances. Some aged into dust. Some became children. Some turned their blades on empty air and begged it to stop wearing their mother’s face.

By nightfall, the army was broken.

Vareen returned alone. Her mace was gone. One side of her face had been burned smooth.

“You were right,” she said.

Ilyas said nothing.

“You also let them die.”

That deserved an answer. He searched for one and found only the truth.

“Yes.”

Vareen laughed once. It was a small, ruined sound. “Then save what is left, monster.”

Ilyas walked down the hill alone.

Chapter VII

The Veiled Sovereign

Karthain did not look like a city when Ilyas entered it. It looked like a memory being revised by someone cruel.

A bridge crossed itself in the air. A fountain poured blood upward into a blackened basin. Houses leaned together like whispering conspirators. The dead lay in patterns too deliberate to be accidental, arranged along the streets in spirals that pointed toward the central square.

Voidborne hunted among the ruins. Ilyas did not hide from them. He walked openly, staff in hand, vestments dark beneath old ash and new rain.

The first pack charged from the shell of a bakery. There were seven of them, each taller than a man, their limbs bending in too many places. Ilyas let them come within reach before opening the Veil.

Their bodies collapsed mid-stride. Momentum carried the remains forward in a smear of bone and shadow.

He did not slow.

At the Avenue of Martyrs, a larger creature unfolded from the side of a chapel. It had stitched the faces of priests into its wings, and each face recited a different prayer in a different voice. Ilyas answered with Light first, more from memory than hope. Radiant fire tore through one wing, freeing three faces into silence.

The creature laughed with the remaining mouths.

Ilyas used Shadow on the rest.

By the time he reached the central square, the rain had stopped falling. Droplets hung motionless in the air, each reflecting a different version of the sky. In one, the sun still shone. In another, Karthain had never been built. In another, Ilyas saw himself as a young priest standing beside Selka’s son, both of them alive, both of them smiling.

He crushed that droplet in his fist.

The Veiled Sovereign waited beneath the Rift.

It was not monstrous in the way demons were monstrous. It had no stable body, no horns, no throne of skulls. It was absence shaped by intention. Limbs formed and unformed around a center too dark to perceive. Eyes opened across its silhouette, then sank back into nothing. When it spoke, every corpse in the square turned its head toward Ilyas.

“You have carried us carefully,” it said.

Ilyas tightened his grip on the staff. “I used what I needed.”

“Yes,” said the Sovereign. “That is what hunger calls restraint.”

The battle began without motion. Pressure struck Ilyas hard enough to shatter the stones behind him. He answered with Radiant Severance, carving a line of Light through the Sovereign’s torso. The wound opened onto stars. Something inside the creature laughed.

Voidborne rose from every corpse in the square.

Ilyas moved through them like a plague with intent. Light in his right hand, Shadow in his left, he burned and drained and broke. For every creature he destroyed, the Sovereign pulled another shape from the dead. For every spell he cast, the Rift widened.

The Sovereign did not fight like an enemy. It fought like a truth correcting a lie.

It showed him Mira crawling across the catacomb floor. It showed him Bren coughing blood into his palms. It showed him Selka reaching for ash. Each vision arrived with the force of a weapon, and each one made the Veil inside him flare in answer.

“You think guilt makes you different from us,” the Sovereign said. “It does not. Guilt is only hunger that has learned to mourn.”

Ilyas drove his staff into the ground. Light spread from the impact in a circle, pure and brilliant, forcing the Voidborne back. For a breath, he saw the square as it had once been: merchants shouting, children running, bells ringing from a tower that still stood.

Then the Light cracked.

He was tired. Not physically. The Veil had made that almost meaningless. He was tired of measuring the cost of each life against the next. Tired of restraint. Tired of pretending balance was noble when balance had only ever meant letting something survive long enough to hurt him again.

The Sovereign leaned close without moving.

“Stop borrowing,” it said. “Be honest.”

Ilyas looked beyond it to the Rift. If it widened, the world would not end quickly. It would be rewritten piece by piece until no one remembered there had been a world to save.

He could continue as he was. He could fight as a man with two powers and lose when one failed to restrain the other.

Or he could stop pretending he was still choosing between them.

Ilyas released the Light.

For the first time, he did not command the Shadow. He did not shape it into a spell or pull it through a narrow wound. He opened every guarded part of himself and let the Veil find no resistance.

The result was not darkness.

It was silence.

The square went still. The hanging droplets fell upward. The remaining Voidborne folded into themselves without sound. The Sovereign recoiled, and in that recoil Ilyas saw fear.

Not because he had become stronger than it.

Because he had become familiar.

Ilyas stepped forward and placed one hand against the Sovereign’s center. The creature tried to expand, to become vast enough to escape being held, but the Veil inside Ilyas was no longer a borrowed thread. It was a door opened from both sides.

He absorbed the Sovereign slowly.

The scream lasted long enough for every bell in Karthain to ring once.

Then the Rift sealed.

Chapter VIII

Aftermath

Dawn came late to Karthain.

When the sun finally rose, it found the city alive in the way a battlefield is alive after the killing stops. People emerged from cellars, wells, crypts, and collapsed houses. Some wept at the sky. Some searched the dead. Some saw Ilyas standing in the central square and fell to their knees.

He hated them for that.

Not because they were wrong. Because they were still close enough to be hurt.

High Inquisitor Vareen survived until morning. Ilyas found her sitting against the base of a broken statue, one hand pressed to the wound in her side. She watched him approach with the calm of someone who had already spent her fear.

“Is it done?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Are you?”

Ilyas did not answer.

Vareen smiled faintly. “That is what I thought.”

He could have healed her. Once, the instinct would have moved faster than thought. Even now, he felt the shape of the spell waiting in him. He could close the wound, steady her heart, pull her back from the edge.

He could also feel the refugees gathering behind him. Children. Mothers. Old men. Survivors with cracked lips and hopeful eyes.

If he reached for Vareen, the Aura would answer. It always answered. Maybe only one person nearby would weaken. Maybe ten. Maybe the child staring at him from behind an overturned cart would simply grow cold and never understand why.

Vareen saw the calculation in his face.

“There he is,” she whispered.

Ilyas stepped back.

The High Inquisitor closed her eyes, and after a while her breathing stopped.

No one thanked him after that.

A woman approached near the south gate with a wounded boy in her arms. The boy’s leg had been crushed beneath stone. His face was gray with shock, but he was alive. The woman fell to her knees in front of Ilyas and begged without words, lifting the child like an offering.

Ilyas looked at the boy. Then at the crowd behind her. Then at the frost forming along the edges of his own shadow.

He turned and walked away.

The woman screamed after him. Others cursed. Someone threw a stone. It struck his shoulder and fell harmlessly to the road.

Ilyas did not look back.

Beyond Karthain, the world was quieter. Fields bent beneath pale wind. Crows avoided the road ahead of him. By noon, the city was only smoke on the horizon.

He no longer sought absolution. He no longer feared condemnation. Good and evil were words for people who could afford company, for those who still believed a clean choice was simply waiting to be found.

Ilyas had saved the world.

Now the world would have to survive him at a distance.

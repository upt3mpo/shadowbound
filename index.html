Chapter I

The Bell That Would Not Ring

Before the Veil, Father Ilyas believed in proximity.

Stand close to the suffering. Lay hands upon them. Let the Light pass through you.

The Cathedral of Dawn was built on that philosophy. Its pillars were wide so the congregation could gather shoulder to shoulder. Its hymns were written in harmonies, never solos.

On the morning the bells failed, the choir had been mid-verse.

The first scream cut through stained glass.

The second shattered it.

Black-veined parishioners clawed at pews, jaws distended, ribs splitting outward as if something inside wanted air.

Ilyas ran toward them. He always ran toward them.

The child he baptized two weeks prior lunged first. Milk teeth blackened. Fingers bone-thin and sharpened.

“Father,” it gurgled, voice layered with something older.

Ilyas raised his hand. White flame consumed the child. Ash coated his lips.

He told himself it was mercy.

More rose. The courtyard became a furnace of prayer and bone. Every burst of Light erased another face he recognized.

The Light responded. But it did not keep pace.

When he collapsed to one knee, he did not pray for strength.

He prayed for efficiency.

Chapter II

The Veil Beneath the Altar

The scriptures described the Veil as absence.

Darkness without purpose.

That was a lie.

The Veil hummed.

It pulsed beneath the cathedral crypt in a fracture of stone like a heartbeat that did not belong to the world.

Ilyas descended alone. Past sealed reliquaries. Past saints whose bones had been polished by centuries of desperate fingers. Past warnings carved so deep into stone that even dust avoided them.

The air grew cold not with temperature, but with distance. As if warmth itself refused to follow.

“You are not meant for this,” whispered memory.

“I am meant to stop it,” he answered.

He pressed his palm into the fracture.

Something inside the blackness pressed back.

Not teeth. Not claws.

Understanding.

Chapter III

The First Borrowing

When the next incursion came, he did not raise both hands.

Only one.

Shadow spilled through his veins like cold water. It did not burn like Light. It settled. It filled empty spaces he had not known existed.

He reached for a Husk and did not sever it.

He pulled.

Its corruption streamed into him, black threads unraveling into his chest. The Husk withered, skin collapsing inward as if time accelerated.

The villagers behind him gasped.

The air around him dimmed.

He felt stronger than he ever had.

Three villagers fainted.

One did not wake.

They called it coincidence. They called him miracle-worker.

They did not stand too close.

Chapter IV

The Party That Faded

The Church refused to abandon him. That would mean admitting error.

They assigned companions.

Sir Albrecht, shield of the Dawn. Mira, novice hymnkeeper. Tovan, mercenary with a laugh too loud for crypts.

They descended into the catacombs together.

The walls pulsed with wet rot. Hymns echoed off stone that seemed to breathe.

Albrecht held the line. Mira sang, voice trembling but true. Tovan cracked jokes until the first corpse on the wall opened its eyes.

Ilyas felt the Rot creeping toward their hearts.

Light alone would not save them.

He widened himself.

The air folded inward. Shadow thickened like tar.

Malgeroth dissolved in convulsing shrieks.

So did Albrecht.

Armor clanged hollow to the stone floor.

Mira’s hymn faltered mid-note. Her skin paled, veins darkening beneath.

She looked at him, not accusing. Confused.

“Father… why am I cold?”

The zone collapsed.

Silence replaced music.

Chapter V

The Social Cost

Inns grew quiet when he entered.

Candles shortened in their holders.

Children developed nosebleeds if he lingered too long.

He learned to sit apart. He learned to speak less. He learned which roads had no beggars on them.

He told himself isolation was discipline.

It was easier than admitting relief.

When alone, his power sharpened. Focused. Efficient.

When alone, no one died unexpectedly.

He began to prefer it.

Chapter VI

The Rift Over Karthain

The sky tore open above the capital.

A wound of endless depth. Something vast pressed against its edges, testing the seam of reality.

The High Inquisitor knelt before him in the cathedral ruins.

“Lead us.”

He looked at the soldiers assembled behind her. Their eyes were steady. Hopeful.

He stepped back.

“No.”

They went without him.

Most did not return.

When the third wave failed, the Inquisitor sought him again.

“You could have prevented this.”

He did not argue.

He walked toward the Rift alone.

Chapter VII

The Veiled Sovereign

The creature inside the tear was not monstrous in form.

It was absence shaped like intention.

Limbs formed and unformed in layered silhouettes. Eyes blinked where none had been moments before.

“You have tasted us,” it said inside his skull.

“I have tasted necessity,” he replied.

The fight broke architecture.

Stone dissolved into black sand. Towers bent inward. Bells rang once, then aged into dust mid-swing.

Light flared from Ilyas’s left hand. Shadow coiled in his right.

Neither sufficed.

He felt the familiar limitation of balance.

Balance required restraint.

Restraint cost lives.

He did not hesitate.

Shadow did not pour into him.

He dissolved into it.

The Sovereign recoiled as Ilyas expanded, no longer conduit but convergence.

He did not strike.

He absorbed.

The creature screamed as its vastness compressed into a singularity within his chest.

The Rift sealed.

The sky healed.

Chapter VIII

Aftermath

He stood in the quiet capital square.

No soldiers flanked him. No priests blessed him.

People watched from a distance.

A woman approached with a wounded child.

He knelt instinctively.

As he reached out, frost crept across the cobblestones.

The child’s breathing steadied.

The woman’s hands began to tremble uncontrollably.

He withdrew before she could collapse.

Understanding settled.

He could still save.

But saving would always cost something near him.

He no longer sought absolution.

He no longer feared condemnation.

He walked beyond the city gates without farewell.

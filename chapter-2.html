Chapter II

The Veil Beneath the Altar

The dead were still being named when Ilyas found the stair.

It had been hidden beneath the old reliquary, sealed under a slab marked with scripture no living priest remembered learning. The masons who built the Cathedral of Dawn had laid the stone before the first bell was raised, before the altar was consecrated, before the city had decided what kind of god it wanted to believe in.

Ilyas discovered it because Selka’s blood had run beneath the altar and refused to dry.

For three nights, he scrubbed the marble himself. For three nights, the stain returned. On the fourth, he followed the black line between the stones and found that it did not end. It descended.

The stair was narrow. The air beneath the cathedral was colder than the crypts, but it did not smell of dust or burial. It smelled of rain before lightning. Of iron held under the tongue. Of a storm waiting behind a wall.

At the bottom waited a door without hinges.

Its surface was made of something that was not stone. It reflected no torchlight. When Ilyas placed his palm against it, the door did not open. It breathed.

He should have turned back then.

Instead, he prayed.

The Light answered him weakly, as if muffled by deep water. Gold flickered around his fingers and died. In the silence that followed, something on the other side of the door noticed him.

It did not speak in words. It pressed meaning directly into the raw places grief had left behind.

Mercy is a gate. Gates can close.

Ilyas staggered back, hand clutched to his chest. He felt no malice from the presence beyond the door. That was worse. Malice could be opposed. Hunger could be judged. This was neither.

It was function.

A force that did not care whether a life was innocent, guilty, beloved, useful, or damned. It cared only that life was movement, pressure, warmth, and that warmth could be redirected.

Ilyas thought of Selka. He thought of the boy becoming human for one breath before the Light erased him. He thought of the twenty civilians behind the eastern doors. He thought of the exact moment choice had become arithmetic.

“Can you save them?” he asked.

The thing behind the door answered with a sensation like fingers closing around a candle flame.

Not save. Preserve.

Ilyas understood the distinction and hated himself for finding comfort in it.

He returned to the nave before dawn. The bells rang above him. Survivors gathered for morning prayer, their faces hollow from mourning and gratitude. Ilyas stood before them with ash still under his nails and a second voice folded beneath his pulse.

He gave the sermon expected of him. He spoke of endurance, of sacrifice, of the Light that still held them.

Beneath every word, the Veil listened.

Narrative Mechanic

Function Without Malice

The Veil does not tempt Ilyas through cruelty. It offers a colder idea: power that can preserve outcomes without preserving innocence.
